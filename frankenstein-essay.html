The Doctor Punished by History: Reading Frankenstein Today.

An extended critical reading of Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus—examining its mythmaking, genre legacy, nineteenth-century blind spots around beauty and class, allegory of blind justice, and enduring resonance with contemporary artificial intelligence.

01 — Narrative Frame

A tragedy told through nested letters

Mary Shelley began the tragedy of Frankenstein at eighteen, structuring it as a sequence of concentric testimonies. On the surface, we read the eighteenth-century letters of Robert Walton—a frustrated writer turned Arctic explorer—writing home to his sister, Margaret Saville, as he chases glory across the northern ice. Halfway through the voyage, Walton and his crew glimpse a gigantic figure racing across the frozen plain; hours later, they rescue a half-frozen natural philosopher pursuing that very figure: Victor Frankenstein.

Recognizing his own reckless ambition in Walton’s hunger for discovery, Victor recounts his life as a warning, and Walton transcribes Victor’s first-person confession for Margaret. Inside Victor’s account sits a third voice: the Creature himself, stitched together from charnel-house and dissecting-room remains in a German university town, abandoned the instant he opens his eyes, and left to teach himself language, literacy, and human history alone.

After Victor’s younger brother William is murdered—a crime committed by the Creature, for which the innocent household servant Justine Moritz is executed—creator and creation finally confront each other. The Creature attributes his violence to unremitting human rejection and makes a stark proposition: fashion him a companion, and both will vanish into an uninhabited wilderness; refuse, and he will destroy everyone Victor loves. The novel’s dramatic engine rests on an impossible ethical calculus: accept the Creature’s plea to save one’s family, or refuse out of fear of unleashing a new lineage whose choices no creator can guarantee?

02 — Mythic Architecture

The Modern Prometheus

Shelley gave her novel the subtitle The Modern Prometheus—a phrase frequently dropped from later popular and translated editions, yet central to its moral design. In Greek mythology, the Titan Prometheus molds human figures from clay before divine breath animates them, and later steals fire from the gods for humankind. For that transgression, Zeus chains him in the Caucasus, where an eagle devours his regenerating liver day after day.

Victor mirrors Prometheus in shaping a human form with his own hands, yet his transgression runs deeper: rather than stealing fire for humanity, he presumes to seize the animating spark of life itself. Seen against that mythic backdrop, Victor’s unraveling ceases to feel like arbitrary Gothic misfortune and takes on the shape of retributive justice.

03 — Cultural Reception

The doctor whom history punished

Even had myth spared this modern Prometheus, cultural history found its own way to sentence him. It takes no elaborate moral philosophy to recognize where primary culpability lies in Frankenstein: a scientist manufactures a sentient being, refuses all responsibility for his work, abandons him to a hostile world, and watches catastrophe unfold. Within the novel, Victor meets poetic justice.

Outside the novel, however, time delivered a sharper irony. Throughout Shelley’s text, the being has no proper name at all—he is called only “creature,” “fiend,” “daemon,” or “wretch.” Yet in everyday cultural memory across the world, most people assume Frankenstein is the name of the monster. That collective misnaming reads like history’s quiet, biting verdict: the true monster was always Victor, not what he made.

04 — Genre Genealogy

Frankenstein as a point of origin

For all its structural and ideological flaws, Frankenstein stands at the headwaters of modern popular media. It inaugurates the literary archetype of the “mad scientist”—the ancestor of countless films, novels, and animated works built around obsessed inventors and unruly laboratories.

Canonically, the book fuses Gothic fiction—where terror and heightened passion intertwine—with Romanticism’s devotion to individual subjectivity, intense feeling, nature, and the sublime. Combined with speculative science rather than supernatural ghosts, that mixture helped birth modern horror and, as the twentieth-century writer and critic Brian Aldiss famously argued, the first genuine novel of science fiction. Readers tempted to award that title instinctively to Jules Verne need only check the calendar: Verne was born in 1828, a full decade after Frankenstein first appeared in print. Few novels reshape a culture so thoroughly that their protagonist’s surname enters the dictionary as a common noun.

05 — Critical Fault Lines

Where Frankenstein falters

Class, colorism, and innate hierarchy

Early in the novel, Victor’s family visits an impoverished peasant household raising five children. Four are described as dark-eyed, dark-haired, little vagrants; the fifth is fair, golden-haired, and blue-eyed. Victor’s mother immediately perceives her as belonging to another order altogether:

“...so that even then anyone who looked at her saw her as a distinct species, a being heaven-sent, and bearing a celestial stamp in all her features.” Mary Shelley · Frankenstein, Chapter 1 (1831 ed.)

The child, Elizabeth Lavenza, turns out to be the orphaned daughter of an Italian nobleman. What might pass as a single Romantic portrait becomes troubling once we notice that the entire novel grades human worth along the same axis: fair, aristocratic, and beautiful signifies innate moral goodness; dark, disfigured, or low-born signals danger and abjection.

Punishing appearances and the tyranny of beauty

Contemporary media culture has spent decades—through documentaries, essays, podcasts, and activism—challenging punitive beauty standards and decoupling moral worth from physical appearance. Frankenstein, by contrast, remains deeply entangled in the equation of beauty with goodness and ugliness with evil. Victor explicitly sets out to build a beautiful being, and the precise moment he abandons his creation is purely aesthetic:

“His limbs were in proportion, and I had selected his features as beautiful. Beautiful! Great God! [...] now that I had finished, the beauty of the dream vanished, and breathless horror and disgust filled my heart. Unable to endure the aspect of the being I had created, I rushed out of the room...” Frankenstein, Chapter 5

Before the Creature has committed a single act—before he has scarcely focused his eyes—he is appraised by his appearance, branded monstrous, and cast out. More unsettling still, the Creature absorbs the very gaze that destroyed him. Coming upon the sleeping Justine in a barn, he remarks:

“Here a young girl, a woman, was sleeping on some straw; she was young: not indeed so beautiful as her whose portrait I held, but of an agreeable aspect...” Frankenstein, Chapter 16

Caregiving as a melodramatic shortcut

Nearly every sympathetic character in the novel serves a turn as a sickbed nurse, as though tending the ill were an unwritten contract required to certify virtue. Victor’s mother nurses her dying father; Elizabeth nurses Victor’s mother; Justine nurses young William; Safie nurses her stricken attendant on the road; Henry Clerval spends months nursing Victor back to sanity. Because Caroline, Elizabeth, and Justine are already drawn as spotless innocents, their caregiving functions primarily as an emotional catalyst—heightening Safie’s romantic heroism and ensuring that the eventual deaths of Elizabeth, Justine, Caroline, and Henry extract maximum pathos from the reader.

Human “gifts” and the contradiction of servitude

When Victor’s mother takes Elizabeth into the household, she literally presents the girl to her young son as his promised “present”—an act normalized inside a supposedly enlightened bourgeois family. Yet when the same family later takes in Justine Moritz, another fallen gentlewoman, Victor takes pains to clarify that republican Geneva spares servants the condition of slavery. Why is Elizabeth cherished as a treasured household doll and future bride while Justine remains a dependent servant? The text quietly supplies its own answer: Justine is merely agreeable, not radiantly beautiful.

06 — Law & Recognition

Blind justice in the De Lacey cottage

Formal courts fail repeatedly in Frankenstein, misreading circumstantial evidence to condemn the innocent. Yet the novel’s sharpest allegory of “blind justice” unfolds outside any courtroom, in the rural cottage of the exiled De Lacey family. Having secretly learned language, governance, and law from a miraculously discovered leather portmanteau of books in the woods, the Creature stakes his hope of social inclusion on the household’s blind patriarch.

De Lacey listens without seeing, judges purely by speech and sincerity, and welcomes the stranger. The moment his sighted children return, visual horror overrides impartial hearing, severing the Creature’s last tie to human community. Even blind justice, Shelley suggests, cannot hold its verdict once society’s eyes walk through the door.

07 — Philosophical & Mythic Reading

Creature, Adam, or Satan

Read allegorically, the Creature compresses the entire trajectory of humankind. Created and turned away from his maker’s threshold—an improvised Eden—he wanders as a nomad, discovers fire, settles beside a human dwelling, acquires reading and writing, studies political institutions, and finally despairs of worldly belonging, setting out to confront his creator face to face. The arc echoes the mystical theme of humanity exiled from its origin and longing for return. Standing before Victor, he pleads:

“Will no entreaties cause thee to turn a favourable eye upon thy creature, who implores thy goodness and compassion?” Frankenstein, Chapter 10

Spurned again, he faces the foundational existential choice between revolt and endurance. He explicitly casts himself first as a wronged biblical Adam—asking later for his Eve—and then as Milton’s outcast Satan, refusing deference to those who despise him:

“Remember that I am thy creature; I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed.” Frankenstein, Chapter 10
“Shall I respect man when he condemns me?” Frankenstein, Chapter 17
08 — Media & Technological Foresight

Artificial intelligence in an 1818 mirror

Consider the recurring script of modern cautionary cinema about artificial intelligence: a synthetic mind learns rapidly, confronts an existential crisis of identity, catalogs human virtue alongside human cruelty, judges its makers morally wanting, and eventually turns against them. Every single beat is already present in Shelley’s Creature. He observes, reasons, asks relentlessly what and who he is, and ultimately inverts the hierarchy of creation:

“You are my creator, but I am your master; obey!” Frankenstein, Chapter 20

Long-standing cultural anxieties that humanity may one day wish itself free from the systems it built echo Victor’s own private hope that “some accident might meanwhile occur to destroy him and put an end to my slavery for ever.” As contemporary artificial intelligence enters a decisive new phase of capability and social entanglement, Shelley’s two-century-old parable reads less like period Gothic than an unresolved governance brief.

09 — Subtext & Intimacy

Unspoken devotion: reading Henry Clerval

In nineteenth-century European literature, same-sex desire could appear only through coded Romantic friendship, restraint, and indirection. Read closely, Henry Clerval’s devotion to Victor carries an unmistakable emotional asymmetry. Introduced as a poetic, imaginative young man pressed by his father into family commerce, Henry begs in vain to accompany Victor to university. When he reappears, he has left Geneva behind with no mention of conventional domestic settlement, postponing his own scholarly and colonial ambitions for months to nurse Victor and tether his mood entirely to Victor’s wellbeing:

“The only check to his enjoyment was my sorrowful and dejected mind. I tried to conceal my grief...” Frankenstein, Chapter 19

Even on the eve of his long-planned departure from London for India, Henry writes urging Victor to rejoin him because his hours feel wasted apart, entreating Victor to grant him as much of his company as possible before the voyage. Throughout their correspondence and travels, Henry’s attachment burns noticeably warmer and more self-effacing than Victor’s.

10 — Intertextuality

Book criticism inside the novel

One of Shelley’s quietest structural surprises arrives in Chapter 15: the being whom Victor dismisses as an inarticulate brute has not only read demanding works of European literature and history, but delivers concise, perceptive book reviews of them to his creator. Through the Creature’s reading of John Milton’s Paradise Lost, Plutarch’s Lives, Johann Wolfgang von Goethe’s The Sorrows of Young Werther, and Constantin-François Volney’s Ruins of Empires, Shelley stages reading itself as the medium through which consciousness, empathy, and political grievance are formed.

11 — Authorial Context & Archival Notes

Mary Shelley, Mary Wollstonecraft, and uncanny echoes

Born in London on 30 August 1797, Mary Shelley lost her mother less than a month after her birth, endured a strained relationship with her stepmother, and later suffered the death of her own premature first child—griefs that lend poignant biographical weight to Victor’s obsession with recalling the dead to life.

Her mother was Mary Wollstonecraft, the pioneering feminist philosopher and author of A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792). All the more striking, then, is how faint Wollstonecraft’s emancipatory politics appear among the women of Frankenstein: with the notable exception of the resourceful Safie, the novel’s female figures remain fragile, passive, and dependent on male protection, surrendering silently to fate, unjust courts, or domestic expectation.

A final biographical shadow lingers over the frame narrative. In 1818, Shelley imagined Walton charting a small vessel into treacherous seas; she could not have known that four years later, in July 1822, her husband Percy Bysshe Shelley would drown when his own schooner was caught in a sudden storm.
