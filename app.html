Dossier 06 · Mobile Media Product & Audience Experience

One app for the whole academy: the Homat Unified Android App.

Product-led and shipped the Homat Unified App (v2.0) — a Persian RTL Android client that unifies a rights-protected library of video courses, e-books and flashcards with an in-app WooCommerce shop, a native blog reader, learner messaging and support — replacing fragmented players and web hand-offs with a single, coherent mobile media product for live paying learners.

01 — Media Product Problem

From scattered touchpoints to one mobile home

Before the unified app, a Homat learner's journey crossed a website catalog, a separate checkout, emailed download links, and standalone player apps — friction that cost sales, generated support requests, and made purchased content feel fragile. For a small independent publisher, every abandoned checkout and every “where is my course?” ticket is a media-management problem, not just a technical one.

I specified a single Android product in which discovery, purchase, delivery and study happen in one Persian, right-to-left environment: the learner opens one app and finds their courses, books and flashcards already there — protected, downloadable for offline study, and kept in sync with the academy's store automatically.

02 — What the Product Unifies

Library, commerce, publishing and support in one client

Protected Personal Library

Each learner's purchased video courses, e-books and flashcard decks in one library, bound to their account and device under the licensing policy of Dossier 04, with offline access to downloaded content.

In-App Shop & Checkout

Courses, books and flashcards are browsable inside the app with prices and ownership status (“already in your library”), handing off to secure WooCommerce payment and returning the purchase to the library automatically.

Native Blog & Messaging

The academy's editorial content is read natively inside the app, and learners receive academy messages and file audited device-change requests without leaving the product.

Audience-First RTL UX

Persian-first, fully right-to-left interface shaped by learner feedback across 38 release iterations — e.g. replacing manual “refresh” controls with silent automatic sync, because real users should never have to think about data freshness.

03 — Built Under Blackout Conditions

Designing for learners the internet could no longer reach

The unified app was not a peacetime redesign. It was built during months of disruption in Iran — protests, periods of direct conflict, a ten-day total internet cutoff and roughly three months of severely restricted connectivity — during which, at times, we could not reach our own hosting and servers. Learners whose courses were already activated on their devices kept studying offline; learners whose access still required an online identity check were locked out until connectivity returned.

Two strategies followed immediately. First, move every online service to domestic hosting: hosting moved to domestic Iranian servers, because international connectivity might be cut again — and because domestic traffic is cheaper and more fairly priced for the students and school learners who make up most of our audience. Second, make everything that can be offline, offline: a redesigned site was prepared from the first day of the cutoff so it could launch the moment the domestic intranet returned, and an in-site ticketing system replaced Telegram — until then our only channel to learners, and suddenly unreachable for us and for them — so existing students could at least reach us about their access.

The unified app is the long-term answer to the same lesson. If search engines and websites can vanish overnight, the learner's courses must already live on their phone. The first release was therefore a protected course player only — blogs and in-app shopping came later; keeping purchased courses reachable came first.

04 — Migration as a Trust Problem

Moving existing learners without breaking a single purchase

The hardest part of the work was the part learners were never meant to notice: migrating learners from the academy's legacy players to the unified app. My non-negotiable product requirements were that existing students must not lose access, must not have to file support tickets, and must not see their device treated as “unknown”. The shipped design therefore transfers each learner's existing entitlements to their new app automatically and in a single all-or-nothing step at first sign-in, with a full audit log — verified live with zero lost licenses, devices or purchases.

Treating migration as an audience-trust exercise rather than a database exercise is, in miniature, the media-management question this portfolio pursues: how independent media ventures keep audiences, revenue and rights intact while their platforms change underneath them.

05 — My Role & Method

Product leadership, honestly framed

I did not hand-write this application's code, and this dossier does not claim software engineering. My work was the product work around it: defining the information architecture and licensing rules, writing acceptance criteria, directing implementation, testing 38 release candidates on real devices, governing the live migration of paying learners, and iterating the Persian RTL interface from learner feedback. That division — editorial and product governance on one side, specialist implementation on the other — is stated transparently here and in my CV, and is precisely the management layer media organisations actually hire for.
